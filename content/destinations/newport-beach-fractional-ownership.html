What is fractional ownership in Newport Beach?

Fractional ownership in Newport Beach means buying a deeded 1/8 share of a luxury California coastal second home — held in a purpose-built LLC alongside up to seven other co-owners. Each owner receives approximately 45 days of personal use per year through a fair-rotation calendar, with all property management, maintenance, taxes and operations handled by a professional team. Where to own in Newport Beach

Newport Beach's second-home market is best understood through four distinct sub-zones, each with its own architecture, harbour or ocean geography, buyer mix and day-to-day rhythm. Together they account for the overwhelming majority of international and domestic second-home demand in greater Newport: Balboa Island and Lido Isle for the quintessential harbour-island experience; the Balboa Peninsula for the direct ocean-strand address; Corona del Mar for clifftop elegance and the most sophisticated village dining on the coast; and Newport Coast and Crystal Cove for the most dramatically sited hillside estates with ocean panoramas that extend from Catalina Island to the San Clemente headland.

Balboa Island and Lido Isle

Balboa Island, the largest of Newport Harbour's man-made islands, is the most quintessentially Californian harbour address in the United States — a compact flat island of approximately 0.2 square miles, linked to the Balboa Peninsula by a bridge and to the harbour's north shore by the Balboa Island Ferry, whose three flat-bottomed car-and-passenger boats have been crossing the harbour mouth every few minutes since 1919. The island's residential character is defined by its village scale: a perimeter walk along the Grand Canal and the outer bay seawall, a dense interior grid of residential lots where single-family homes with private docks front directly onto the harbour's inner channels, and a Main Street retail strip — Marine Avenue — that delivers the kind of walkable ice-cream-shop, boutique and harbour-view cafe rhythm that the American coastal second-home imagination has been constructing for most of the twentieth century. Properties on the outer bay frontage of Balboa Island — the stretch along South Bayfront and the seawall walk — command some of the most durable waterfront premiums in Orange County, with private docks capable of accommodating vessels up to forty feet providing direct harbour access from the garden gate.

The cultural geography of Balboa Island is remarkably stable. The Newport Beach Christmas Boat Parade — running annually since 1908, one of the longest-running boat parades in the United States — routes its illuminated fleet through the harbour channels directly past the island's bayfront homes, with island residents hosting viewing parties on their docks and seawalls each December. The Balboa Bay Club and the Balboa Yacht Club, both on the bay's northern shore near the island's bridgehead, anchor a sailing and racing calendar that runs through most of the year. The island's restaurant scene is the most walkable in the harbour — a density of dining within the island perimeter that larger suburban beach towns cannot replicate — and the absence of through-traffic (the only reason to drive onto Balboa Island is to be there) gives the streets a pedestrian character exceptional for Southern California. The classic frozen banana, a chocolate-dipped banana on a stick sold from Marine Avenue stands since the 1940s, has long since escaped its local origins to become a regional cultural reference; residents treat it with appropriate affection.

Lido Isle, immediately west of Balboa Island across the harbour's main channel, is the harbour's most architecturally cohesive residential island — a private island of approximately 0.2 square miles whose homes wrap a communal central park and whose architectural code, though less rigorously enforced than Coral Gables or the older California beach towns, has produced a consistency of Mediterranean-Revival and Spanish Colonial styles that gives the island a visual coherence unusual in California residential development. Lido Isle's most distinctive features are the Lido Isle Community Association beachfront — a private sandy beach on the harbour side, accessible to island residents and their guests — and the deep-water frontage of the island's southern and eastern perimeter, where properties with direct harbour access command some of the most concentrated premiums in the Newport market. The island is connected to the mainland by a single bridge at Via Lido, placing the Newport Beach retail and restaurant infrastructure of Lido Marina Village and the bay immediately adjacent. Drive times from Balboa Island and Lido Isle to John Wayne Airport (SNA) are 15–20 minutes; to Fashion Island 10–15 minutes; to Corona del Mar 10 minutes; to Crystal Cove 20 minutes. Climate on the harbour islands runs at a consistently maritime 14–19°C (high 50s°F to high 60s°F) in winter, with the bay's thermal mass moderating the overnight lows, and 21–25°C (low-to-mid-70s°F) in summer, with the marine layer keeping the harbour surface cooler than inland Orange County by a reliable 5–8°C (9–14°F) on most summer afternoons. Best for: sailing families and yachting buyers who want private dock access and a year-round harbour culture, walkable-village devotees who value the Marine Avenue pedestrian rhythm, buyers drawn to the Christmas Boat Parade and the annual harbour racing calendar, and multi-generational families for whom the island's car-free pedestrian scale provides a genuine respite from the surrounding urban freeway landscape.

The Balboa Peninsula

The Balboa Peninsula, the long narrow sandspit running west to east between the ocean strand and the harbour's southern shore, is Newport Beach's direct Pacific frontage — a three-and-a-half-mile barrier beach whose ocean side faces the full force of the Southern California swells and whose harbour side looks north across the bay toward Balboa Island and the city's skyline. The Peninsula divides into two distinct residential characters. The ocean-front strip along Ocean Front Walk — a wide pedestrian boardwalk running the full length of the ocean side — holds the most coveted direct-beach positions, with a mix of older beach-cottage character (the tight-lotted legacy of Newport's early-twentieth-century beach culture) and a newer class of high-specification contemporary beach villas built on the standard twenty-five-foot lots to the maximum allowable height. The harbour-side strip, along Bay Front and the interior channels, holds the Peninsula's boating community — smaller vessels than the main harbour's deep-water fleet, but direct bay access from homes with waterfront orientation.

The Peninsula's architectural heritage is the most layered in Newport Beach. The Balboa Pavilion — a Victorian-era waterfront structure with its distinctive pointed cupola, built in 1905 as a streetcar terminus and still standing at the harbour-side of the Peninsula's mid-point — is the harbour's most recognisable landmark and a California Historical Landmark whose preservation reflects the city's investment in its own visual identity. The Balboa Fun Zone immediately around the Pavilion — the Ferris wheel visible from the harbour, the arcade and waterfront dining — gives the mid-Peninsula a family-resort character that has been present in some form since the city's founding as a beach destination in the 1900s. The ocean-side tip of the Peninsula, at The Wedge, is the most famous bodysurfing break on the California coast — a shore-break wave that bounces off the east jetty and produces the largest near-shore surf in Southern California on the right south swell. It attracts specialists rather than casual swimmers, but its reputation as a spectacle is considerable and the gathering of observers on the east jetty on a big-swell day is one of the harbour's reliable free entertainments. Drive times from the Peninsula to SNA are 20–25 minutes; to the harbour's north shore 10 minutes; to Corona del Mar via the Balboa Ferry and a short drive 20 minutes. Climate runs 13–18°C (high 50s°F to mid-60s°F) in winter to 21–25°C (low-to-mid-70s°F) in summer, cooled further than the harbour by the direct ocean exposure and the prevailing westerly sea breeze that picks up through the afternoon hours. Best for: ocean-facing buyers who want the direct Pacific-strand experience and the surf culture of The Wedge, beach-boardwalk enthusiasts who value the walkable pedestrian scale of Ocean Front Walk, families with older children drawn to the beach sports and seasonal festival calendar along the Peninsula, and buyers for whom the Balboa Pavilion's historical character and the harbour's recreational boating scene provide a genuinely distinctive California coastal experience.

Corona del Mar

Corona del Mar — "Crown of the Sea" — is the most architecturally refined and consistently well-regarded residential village on the Newport Beach coastline, occupying the bluffs south-east of the harbour mouth and the canyon-cut terrain descending to the Pacific between the harbour jetty and Reef Point. The village's street plan, laid out in the 1920s on a grid of flower-named streets — Dahlia, Jasmine, Marguerite, Poppy, Goldenrod, Larkspur — rising from the ocean bluff to the Pacific Coast Highway, gives the area a botanical regularity that is immediately legible as the work of a developer who had the European hill-village in mind as a model. The architecture that has occupied those lots ranges from the original 1920s–1930s California bungalow stock, through the post-war coastal modernism that produced many of the neighbourhood's best single-family homes, to the contemporary high-specification beach villas that have replaced older stock on the premium ocean-view lots along Ocean Boulevard, the clifftop street running along the bluff edge above Big Corona State Beach.

Big Corona State Beach, immediately below the bluff, is one of the best-maintained and most consistently awarded public beaches in Orange County — a wide south-facing strand protected on the west by the harbour's east jetty and on the east by the rocky reef of Reef Point, giving it a calmer and more sheltered character than the exposed Peninsula beaches to its west. The tide pools at Reef Point, accessible at low tide from the beach's eastern end, are among the most accessible and richest intertidal ecosystems on the California coast, administered by the California Department of Parks and Recreation and protected as a State Marine Conservation Area. The village retail strip on East Coast Highway and the perpendicular flower-named streets is the most concentrated luxury-dining cluster in Newport Beach — Five Crowns, The Quiet Woman, Zinc Café, Rothschild's and a cluster of wine bars and contemporary-cuisine restaurants — giving the village a year-round evening restaurant economy that outpaces its residential scale. Drive times from Corona del Mar to SNA are 15–20 minutes; to Fashion Island 10 minutes; to Crystal Cove 5–10 minutes; to the harbour's north shore 15 minutes. Climate runs 14–19°C (high 50s°F to high 60s°F) in winter to 22–26°C (low-to-mid-70s°F) in summer, with the bluff exposure providing reliable afternoon sea breezes that keep the village noticeably cooler than the inland Orange County basin. Best for: architectural-quality buyers drawn to the flower-named-streets village character and the clifftop ocean views, fine-dining enthusiasts who value the concentrated village restaurant scene, families and couples who want the combination of Big Corona Beach, the tide-pool ecology and the sophisticated village retail within walking distance of the property, and buyers for whom a Catalina Island view from a morning coffee terrace is a legitimate lifestyle criterion.

Newport Coast and Crystal Cove

Newport Coast, the hillside residential district rising above the Pacific Coast Highway between Corona del Mar and Laguna Beach, is the most dramatically sited and architecturally ambitious of Newport Beach's four primary sub-zones — a community of large-lot estates and gated residential enclaves built into the bluffs, canyons and ridgelines of the Irvine Coast between 100 and 300 metres above sea level, with unobstructed Pacific panoramas extending south along the coast from the San Clemente headland to the San Nicolas Island silhouette on the clearest winter days. The hillside topography, protected from ocean development on the coast side by the Crystal Cove State Park land-use designation, gives the Newport Coast estates a combination of ocean-view elevation and privacy that the flat harbour and Peninsula properties cannot provide — a different ownership experience from the harbour-community character of Balboa Island or the village-street character of Corona del Mar, and a richer one for buyers whose primary criterion is the view rather than the harbour access.

Crystal Cove State Park, the 3.2-mile protected beach and 2,400-acre hillside parkland immediately adjacent to Newport Coast, is one of the most significant coastal state parks in Southern California — a landscape that combines an accessible Pacific beach, a colony of forty-six historic 1920s–1940s beach cottages that can be reserved through the state park system, and a marine conservation area that protects the offshore kelp beds and rocky reef ecosystems. The park boundary effectively functions as a permanent open-space easement on the west side of the Newport Coast estate district, preventing the residential development of the hillside slopes that back up to the park edge and preserving the park's coastal scrub and native grassland habitat under the California State Parks mandate. The practical consequence for Newport Coast residents is that the hillside views from their estates include both the Pacific Ocean and the undeveloped coastal parkland — a combination that is, in Southern California's heavily developed coastal strip, genuinely exceptional. Pelican Hill Resort, the Irvine Company's flagship coastal hospitality property on the Newport Coast ridgeline, offers two Tom Fazio–designed championship golf courses, a Roman-amphitheatre-style pool, and a spa and restaurant calendar that supplements the neighbourhood's own private amenity stock. Drive times from Newport Coast to SNA are 15–20 minutes; to Fashion Island 10–15 minutes; to Laguna Beach 10–15 minutes. Climate at elevation runs 11–17°C (low-to-mid-50s°F to mid-60s°F) in winter to 21–25°C (low-to-mid-70s°F) in summer, with the hillside position typically 3–5°C warmer than the harbour-level islands in the coldest months but cooled through the summer afternoons by the onshore sea breeze rising up the canyon-cut slopes. Best for: buyers who prioritise the panoramic Pacific view above harbour access, golf and resort amenity devotees, nature-oriented owners who want the Crystal Cove hiking trails and beach directly accessible, large-format estate buyers who value privacy and lot size above walkable village scale, and design-led couples for whom a Pelican Hill sunrise over the Pacific is a sufficient lifestyle argument on its own.

A year in your Newport Beach co-ownership home

Newport Beach's particular value as a fractional second home derives in large part from its genuine year-round usability — a coastal city whose harbour and ocean calendar, mild marine climate and event density give an owner usable weeks in every month rather than the compressed peak-season window that characterises most American beach destinations. Spreading 45 days of use across a Newport Beach year is, for most owners, an easier assignment than they expect: the challenge is choosing between the options rather than filling the calendar. The fair-rotation scheduling across all co-owners ensures that every owner receives access to peak weeks — the Christmas-and-New-Year harbour window, the summer high season, the spring-event calendar — on an equitable basis over the ownership period.

Spring (March–May)

Newport Beach's spring season is, in the subjective experience of most longtime harbour users, the single best weather window of the year. March temperatures on the coast run at 15–20°C (high 50s°F to high 60s°F) by day, the winter marine-layer pattern is beginning to lift, and the harbour's sailing community is shifting from the winter-racing calendar into the spring-cruise season. The Newport Beach Yacht Club and the Balboa Yacht Club run spring racing series through March, April and May that give the harbour a consistently animated sailing backdrop; the Newport Harbor Yacht Club's spring programme includes the Pacific Coast One Design championships and the Southern California Racing Association's regional fleet racing, drawing competitive sailing from across the state. The annual Newport to Ensenada International Yacht Race, one of the oldest and largest offshore sailboat races in the world — first run in 1948 under the auspices of the Newport Ocean Sailing Association — departs from the harbour entrance in late April, filling the bay approaches with several hundred offshore racers in one of the most visually spectacular starts in American ocean racing.

April and May bring the spring wildflower bloom to the Crystal Cove hillside trails — Californian poppies, lupins, sage-scrub wildflowers — and the coastal sage scrub of the Irvine Coast park boundary turns a particular shade of silver-green that marks the dry-season transition. The Newport Beach Film Festival, one of the top-ten film festivals in the United States by attendance and submission volume, runs through late April, filling the city's cinema venues and the outdoor screenings along the harbour with an international film programme alongside the domestic competition. Daytime temperatures by mid-May reach 20–24°C (high 60s°F to mid-70s°F), the water temperature on the strand is approaching 17–18°C (low-to-mid-60s°F) — cool for swimming but comfortable for paddleboarding, kayaking and open-water swimming in wetsuits — and the harbour is at its most active before the full summer visitor pressure arrives. For co-ownership owners allocating weeks, a well-timed April or May week can combine the Film Festival, the spring sailing calendar, the wildflower coastal hiking and the best daytime temperatures of the year without the high-season crowds of July and August. The Orange County Museum of Art, now in its Thom Mayne–designed building in the civic centre, typically opens major exhibition cycles in spring, giving owners a serious contemporary-art programme to anchor the cultural end of a week's visit.

Summer (June–August)

Newport Beach's summer season is the most reliably celebrated of any California coastal destination — not because the temperatures are dramatic (the marine layer that rolls in most summer mornings ensures that 23–27°C (mid-70s°F) is the coastal maximum rather than the inland triple-digit heat) but because the combination of warm harbour water, long evenings and a fully activated leisure calendar produces an intensity of enjoyment that distinguishes it from more passive beach destinations. June opens with the Duffy boat season — the electric pontoon boats that are Newport Harbour's signature leisure craft, rented from dozens of operators along the harbour shore and used for sunset dinner cruises, harbour tours and private evening parties — and the harbour's restaurant cluster at Lido Marina Village and the eastern bayfront hits its annual peak traffic. Sea temperatures on the harbour in June reach 18–20°C (mid-to-high-60s°F) — comfortable for paddleboarding and harbour swimming, especially in the protected coves of the inner channels.

July and August are the absolute high season on the Peninsula and the harbour — daytime temperatures holding at 23–26°C (mid-70s°F), ocean temperatures on the Pacific strand reaching 19–22°C (high 60s°F), the beach-and-boardwalk economy at its peak. The Fourth of July brings Newport Beach's most elaborately mounted municipal fireworks display, launched from the harbour with viewing positions along the bay from Balboa Island's seawall to the Balboa Peninsula boardwalk — one of the most atmospheric holiday celebrations on the Southern California coast. The summer sailing calendar includes the Newport to Catalina Island race in July and the Bay to Bay summer series run by the harbour's yacht clubs, while the Pacific strand from Corona del Mar to the Peninsula holds some of the summer's best bodysurf and longboard conditions as the south swells from Polynesian storm systems arrive on the coast. The Newport Beach Restaurant Week, typically running in late August, provides a curated access point to the city's deep dining inventory at accessible price points — a useful week for owners exploring the neighbourhood's culinary depth rather than returning to known venues. For owners with family groups including teenagers, summer in Newport Beach — beach sports, harbour kayaking, Duffy boat excursions, the Fashion Island evening retail circuit — is among the most effortlessly usable summer weeks available in American coastal second-home markets.

Autumn (September–November)

September and October are, for many experienced Newport Beach second-home owners, the favourite months of the year. The summer visitor pressure has resolved; the ocean temperatures are at their annual peak — 20–22°C (high 60s°F to low 70s°F) on the Pacific strand in September, the warmest of any month — and the daytime temperatures settle into a 20–24°C (high 60s°F to mid-70s°F) band that combines warm sunshine with the low humidity and clear-sky visibility that distinguish October in Southern California from any other time of year. The Santa Ana wind pattern, which sweeps offshore across the coast from the desert in October and November, periodically delivers days of exceptional clarity — the Catalina Island silhouette sharpens to a hard edge, the Palos Verdes Peninsula and even the San Gabriel Mountains become visible from the harbour, and the ocean takes on a deep blue-indigo colour characteristic of the Santa Ana weather window that is among the most dramatic meteorological spectacles in the American West.

October is the Crystal Cove hiking month — the chaparral on the hillside trails turns its autumnal grey-green, the coastal sage scrub fills with late-season wildflowers, and the offshore kelp beds are at their clearest for snorkelling and diving in the marine conservation area below the bluff. The autumn sailing calendar includes the Southern California Racing Association's end-of-season regatta series and the Newport Beach Commodores Cup weekend that closes the racing season in the harbour. November brings the city's restaurant and cultural season back into full operation — the harbour-side dining, the Orange County Museum of Art autumn exhibitions, the Segerstrom Center's autumn performing-arts programme — and the Christmas Boat Parade preparation begins in mid-November as the harbour's boat owners start rigging their illuminated displays. For owners who travel flexibly and want to see Newport Beach in its most authentic and unhurried state, a late October or early November week delivers the best ocean temperatures of the year, the clearest skies, and a city running at its own pace rather than the high-season visitor rhythm.

Winter (December–February)

Newport Beach's winter season is defined by the Newport Beach Christmas Boat Parade — one of the longest-running and most consistently spectacular boat parades in the United States, running every evening for five nights in mid-December through the harbour's main channels, with hundreds of illuminated vessels (from kayaks to sixty-foot motor yachts) and the bayfront homes of Balboa Island and the Peninsula competing for the parade trophy with their own shore-based light displays. The Boat Parade has been running annually since 1908 and is organised by the Commodore's Fleet; the most coveted viewing positions are the bayfront homes of Balboa Island's South Bayfront Walk, the seawall of the Balboa Peninsula and the dining decks of the harbour-side restaurants, all of which fill days in advance of the parade nights. For a co-ownership owner with a harbour-facing property, the December Boat Parade week is among the most genuinely distinctive single-event experiences available in the American coastal second-home calendar — an event that has no direct equivalent at any other California coastal address.

January and February are the quietest months in Newport Beach by visitor volume and the most comfortable by the judgement of many year-round residents. The daytime temperatures run at 16–20°C (low-to-mid-60s°F) — warm enough for outdoor dining, coastal hiking and harbour walking, cool enough to enjoy without the summer humidity buildup. The whale-watching season peaks in January and February as grey whale pods pass the Newport Coast on their annual southward migration from Arctic feeding grounds to Baja California breeding lagoons — a phenomenon that makes the waters off Crystal Cove and the harbour entrance some of the most reliably cetacean-rich in California in those months. The Segerstrom Center for the Arts in Costa Mesa, a fifteen-minute drive from the harbour, is in full season through January and February with Broadway touring productions, the Pacific Symphony's winter programme and the Dance at the Segerstrom spring cycle. The harbour's sailing clubs run their winter series, the racecourse courses on the inside bay are used for dinghy racing and junior sailing through the lighter-wind winter months, and the city's restaurant and retail infrastructure — Fashion Island, the Corona del Mar village strip, Lido Marina Village — operates at its full year-round capacity. Winter in Newport Beach is not an off-season; it is a quieter version of the same city, operating at a pace that owners who cannot get peak weeks often end up valuing more than the high-season alternatives.

California property taxes and Proposition 13

California property tax is administered by individual counties; Newport Beach properties sit in Orange County, administered by the Orange County Assessor. The defining structural feature of California's property-tax regime is Proposition 13, passed by California voters in 1978, which limits the assessed value of a property to its purchase price and caps annual assessment increases at 2% per year regardless of market value movements, until the property is sold and reassessed at the new purchase price. For a Newport Beach fractional buyer, the practical consequence is that the property tax in the year of purchase is based on the LLC's acquisition cost — typically a meaningful discount to current market value on older properties that have been held for some years — and will grow by no more than 2% per year thereafter. California does not impose a state estate tax or inheritance tax, which is a structural advantage for non-resident buyers relative to several other states. Federal estate-tax provisions apply to US-sited assets for non-US-citizen buyers, and a specialist US tax attorney should advise on the specifics before completion. The LLC holds the property, and the annual property tax is paid at the LLC level as part of the consolidated service charge, so individual owners never deal with the county assessor directly.

Beyond property tax, the other principal carry-cost items on a Newport Beach waterfront property are building insurance (California coastal properties carry earthquake and flood components in addition to standard homeowners' coverage), homeowners' association or harbour-association fees (applicable on most Balboa Island and Lido Isle properties and on Newport Coast gated communities), dock and berth fees where the property includes private water access, and the full property-management retainer. All of these are handled at the LLC level, with individual owners seeing only the consolidated annual service-charge figure.

The California Coastal Commission and what it means for property owners

The California Coastal Commission — established under the California Coastal Act of 1976 — is the state regulatory body with authority over development, renovation and land use along the California coastline, including Newport Beach, Corona del Mar and Crystal Cove. Its permit requirements apply to any development or significant modification of property within the coastal zone, which broadly encompasses all land within a designated distance of the mean high tide line. For second-home buyers, the Commission's primary impact is on renovation and construction: any material alteration to the exterior of a coastal-zone property, addition of floor area or changes to the property's massing typically requires a Coastal Development Permit in addition to the standard city building permit. This is not a barrier to ownership or renovation — it is a process, and a well-established one managed routinely by the architecture and construction professionals active in Newport Beach — but it is a constraint that explains, in part, why the architectural character of the Newport Beach coastal zone has remained more stable over the past fifty years than comparable coastal communities without the Commission's jurisdiction. For fractional owners, major renovation decisions are taken by the LLC's annual meeting and managed by the professional management team, which has existing relationships with the relevant permitting authorities.

Newport Beach Fractional Ownership — Frequently Asked Questions

What is fractional ownership in real estate?

Fractional ownership in real estate means co-owning a property with a small group of buyers, where each owner holds a deeded share — typically 1/8. Unlike timeshares, fractional ownership gives you actual recorded property equity, shared appreciation rights, and usage of approximately 45 days per year. All maintenance, management and operating costs are professionally handled and split among co-owners. The share is transferable and inheritable — it is not a use-right that expires, it is a genuine stake in the underlying property. For non-US buyers, the LLC structure means the asset passing on death is the membership interest rather than a direct US real-property interest, which typically simplifies the succession planning considerably relative to direct title ownership.

What is the difference between Balboa Island and Corona del Mar?

Balboa Island is a harbour island experience — flat, walkable, with private docks on the harbour channels, the Balboa Island Ferry, Marine Avenue's village retail and the annual Christmas Boat Parade passing the bayfront homes. It is the quintessential American harbour-village, focused on the protected bay water and the community of boats and people who use it. Properties here typically include harbour frontage and dock access.

Corona del Mar is a clifftop ocean experience — the flower-named streets rising above Big Corona State Beach, the Pacific Ocean views from Ocean Boulevard, the concentrated fine-dining village of East Coast Highway. CdM is more architecturally varied and more restaurant-focused than Balboa Island, with larger lots and dramatic coastal topography rather than the island's flat walkable scale. Buyers choosing between the two are essentially choosing between protected harbour culture and open-ocean panoramic living — both exceptional, meaningfully different.
