What is fractional ownership in Miami?

Fractional ownership in Miami means buying a deeded 1/8 share of a luxury subtropical-urban second home — held in a purpose-built LLC alongside up to seven other co-owners. Each owner receives approximately 45 days of personal use per year through a fair-rotation calendar, with all property management, maintenance, taxes and operations handled by a professional team. It is real, recorded property equity in your name — not a timeshare, not a holiday club.

Why Miami?

Miami is, by a long way, the single most internationally connected coastal city in the United States, and the only American second-home market that functions simultaneously as a Latin-American gateway, a domestic snowbird capital, a global finance node and an international art-and-design destination. The city has been the chosen second-home destination for North-Eastern and Mid-Western American buyers — and increasingly for Latin-American, European and Canadian buyers — for more than seventy years, and the cumulative depth of professional services, multilingual management infrastructure and high-specification residential stock is materially deeper here than in almost any other American sun-belt market. The greater Miami area combines a coastline of close to 135 kilometres (84 miles) wrapping every form of subtropical shore (the long Atlantic strand of Miami Beach, the protected Biscayne Bay, the cove-cut Key Biscayne headland, the inland waterways of the Intracoastal), a year-round subtropical climate warmer in winter than any other major US city, two of the busiest international airports in the Americas at Miami International (MIA) and Fort Lauderdale–Hollywood (FLL), and a cosmopolitan urban core whose financial, art and design districts have emerged in the past two decades as one of the most genuinely international city centres in the Americas. Few other American cities — arguably none south of New York — offer that range of climate, culture and connectivity inside a single half-hour radius.

Miami's particular advantage inside the American second-home market is the combination of climate, connectivity and capital depth that no other US city replicates. The National Weather Service Miami forecast office records average January highs of 25°C (77°F) and lows above 18°C (65°F) — comfortably the mildest mainland-US winter climate of any major city — and a frost-free record going back to the city's founding. The high-summer peak runs 31–33°C (high 80s°F to low 90s°F), moderated by the Biscayne National Park waters and the prevailing easterly trade winds off the Atlantic. That climate pairs with a federal-state tax regime that has drawn the largest internal-migration wave in modern American history: Florida has no state personal income tax, no state estate tax and no inheritance tax, under the Florida Department of Revenue framework — a structural advantage that has accelerated the movement of high-income individuals, financial firms and family offices from New York, New Jersey, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Illinois and California through the past decade. The financial inflows have visibly remade the city's downtown, Brickell and Edgewater skylines, and the residential market underpinning a Miami fractional share is structurally better-supported today than at any prior point in its history.

It is worth setting Miami in its American competitive context. Palm Beach and Naples, the state's senior wealth enclaves, are smaller, quieter and more seasonal, with thinner year-round restaurant and cultural calendars than central Miami; both work as winter-only destinations rather than the year-round urban centre Miami has become. The Hamptons and Nantucket are higher-prestige Atlantic summer addresses but lock the property to a three-month usable window, with empty winters and the carry-cost penalty that implies. Aspen, Vail and Park City compete for the same domestic luxury second-home dollar but are seasonal in the opposite direction, with December through March as the prime quarter. Los Angeles and the California coast offer comparable year-round climate but at materially higher entry prices and on the opposite seaboard from the Latin-American gateway. 30A and the Florida Keys, the state's beach-and-island alternatives, are quieter and more vernacular but lack the international airport connectivity and the urban depth that make Miami practically usable from a Northern-European base. None of these comparisons makes Miami categorically "better" — the right answer depends on the specific buyer's priorities — but they help frame why Miami remains, by a meaningful margin, the most cosmopolitan year-round second-home market in the United States.

The third structural argument for Miami is the diversity of usable lifestyles available inside a single urban region. A family with a 1/8 share in Brickell is a fifteen-minute drive from the long Atlantic strand of Miami Beach, twenty minutes from the cycling and sailing community of Coconut Grove, twenty-five minutes from the Mediterranean-Revival residential streets of Coral Gables, and ten minutes from the contemporary-art galleries of the Design District and Wynwood; a Miami Beach apartment owner is ten minutes from the bayside dining of Bal Harbour and twenty minutes from the contemporary-design quarter of Edgewater; a Coral Gables villa owner is forty-five minutes from the bay-front quiet of Key Biscayne. The city packs a remarkable amount of difference into a single twenty-by-twenty-mile footprint — high-rise urban, Art Deco beach, Mediterranean-Revival residential, bohemian-sailing, contemporary-art quarter, protected island park — and a Miami share that combines proximity to several of these gives an owner a year-round urban base rather than a single-purpose property. Few other American coastal cities can match that range without significantly longer drive times.

For a co-ownership buyer thinking strategically rather than just emotionally, Miami's combination of climate, capital flows and connectivity matters more than the headline glamour. The Brickell tower you buy a share of sits in a financial district whose tenant base — global banks, Latin-American family offices, the largest concentration of international bank branches in the United States after New York — has been building for forty years. The Miami Beach apartment looks out over a strand whose Art Deco Historic District has been protected since its National Register listing in 1979, the largest concentration of 1920s–1940s Art Deco architecture anywhere in the world. The Coral Gables villa sits inside George Merrick's 1925 Mediterranean-Revival masterplan, one of the most thoroughgoing pre-war planned communities in America. The Coconut Grove home is in a neighbourhood that pre-dates the city of Miami itself. These are not assets that depend on a particular interest-rate cycle to hold their value; they depend on the unchanging facts that Miami remains the United States' Latin-American gateway, that the Atlantic keeps washing the same protected shoreline, and that Florida's tax regime keeps doing what it has done for half a century. Add the modern LLC ownership infrastructure that makes shared ownership transparent, taxable and resaleable, and the case for co-ownership in Miami writes itself.

One under-discussed advantage that becomes obvious once you actually start using a Miami second home is the depth of the city's professional services infrastructure for non-resident owners. Seventy years of Northern American, Latin American and increasingly European buyers have built up an ecosystem of multilingual lawyers, property managers, tax advisers, title companies, architects, contractors and notaries in the city that smaller Sun-Belt alternatives simply cannot match. The local management companies in Brickell, Miami Beach, Coral Gables and Coconut Grove operate in Spanish, English, Portuguese, French, Italian and German as a matter of routine, with decades of operating history; the Spanish-speaking community is the city's largest cultural cohort, with full-service legal, medical and financial infrastructure running in two languages. The deed system gives ownership documentary clarity through the Miami-Dade Clerk of Courts recording office, and the property-tax records are administered by the Miami-Dade Property Appraiser on a long-running, reliable, publicly searchable basis. None of this is glamorous, but it is the kind of infrastructure that determines whether owning a second home from another country is a pleasure or a chore.

The fourth structural advantage worth naming is the transport infrastructure that makes a Miami second home practically usable rather than just nominally owned. Miami International Airport (MIA) is the busiest US airport for international passengers after New York JFK, with year-round nonstop service from London, Madrid, Paris, Frankfurt, Amsterdam, Zurich, Rome, Milan, Lisbon, Istanbul, Buenos Aires, São Paulo, Bogotá, Lima, Santiago, Mexico City, Toronto and a dense web across every major Caribbean and Central-American hub, supplemented by domestic service from every significant US city. Fort Lauderdale–Hollywood (FLL), forty-five minutes north, adds further low-cost capacity, and the Brightline high-speed rail line — which opened its Miami–Orlando extension in 2023 — connects central Miami to West Palm Beach in seventy minutes and Orlando in three and a half hours, by far the most usable inter-city rail line in the American south-east. Most Northern European hubs are under nine hours' flight time; East-Coast US cities are two to three hours; Latin-American capitals are three to six hours. An owner can leave London on a Friday morning and be having dinner on Lincoln Road that evening; can leave New York at 7am and be in Brickell for an 11am meeting; can fly in from São Paulo for a long weekend without changing time zones.

The fifth and least-discussed structural advantage of Miami is the depth of the city's cultural infrastructure, which has matured to a degree that surprises buyers expecting a beach-and-bar destination. The Pérez Art Museum Miami, opened on the bay in 2013 in a Herzog & de Meuron building suspended on stilts above the water, is among the most distinguished contemporary-art museums in the southern United States; Frost Science Museum next door delivers the city's principal science-and-aquarium anchor. The Adrienne Arsht Center for the Performing Arts programmes a year-round symphony, opera, ballet and Broadway-touring calendar in César Pelli's downtown complex; the New World Symphony on Miami Beach runs the most active classical-music education programme in the southern United States in its Frank Gehry-designed building. The Institute of Contemporary Art Miami in the Design District and the de la Cruz Collection pair with the Bass Museum on Miami Beach and the Vizcaya Museum and Gardens on the bay to form an art-and-architecture trail that takes a serious visitor three or four days to walk. Behind the museum infrastructure sits one of the most active contemporary architecture programmes in the United States — Herzog & de Meuron, Frank Gehry, César Pelli, Norman Foster, Bjarke Ingels, Ateliers Jean Nouvel, Renzo Piano, Zaha Hadid Architects, Carlos Ott and Arquitectonica all hold significant Miami buildings — and the cumulative effect is a city that has been substantially rebuilt as a contemporary architectural showcase since the early 2000s.

Where to own in Miami

Miami's second-home market is best understood through five distinct sub-zones, each with its own architecture, microclimate, density and buyer mix. There are, of course, Miami addresses outside these five — Bal Harbour and the long Sunny Isles strand to the north, Fisher Island for the ultra-discreet billionaire community, Aventura for the family-suburb dimension, South Miami and Pinecrest for the leafy residential interior, the Florida Keys reaching south from the city — and we are happy to discuss them with buyers whose interests run that direction. But the supply story for fractional ownership is concentrated in the five clusters below: Brickell for the financial-district high-rise, Miami Beach for the Atlantic-strand condominium and Art Deco apartment, Coconut Grove for the bohemian-luxury sailing village, Coral Gables for the Mediterranean-Revival residential interior, and the Edgewater–Design District–Wynwood corridor for the contemporary-art quarter. Together they account for the overwhelming majority of international second-home demand in greater Miami.

Brickell

Brickell is, on most measures, the most cosmopolitan financial district anywhere in the southern United States — a one-square-mile peninsula immediately south of the Miami River, sitting between the downtown skyline and the protected waters of Biscayne Bay, with one of the densest concentrations of high-specification residential towers in the Americas. Through the past two decades the district has built up the largest concentration of international bank branches in the United States outside New York, with global, Latin-American and European institutions clustered along Brickell Avenue and Brickell Bay Drive; the resident population has roughly tripled since 2010, and the streetscape has matured from a daytime banking quarter to a genuinely twenty-four-hour mixed-use neighbourhood. The prime residential addresses sit along the bayfront strip (Brickell Avenue south of the river, the Brickell Bay Drive towers, the protected enclave of Brickell Key reachable by a single bridge) and along the inland spine (Brickell Avenue itself, Coral Way to the south, the cluster around Brickell City Centre) where contemporary glass-and-steel residential towers have been built into the urban fabric over the past fifteen years.

Brickell's anchor cultural institutions sit a few minutes from the residential towers: the Adrienne Arsht Center for the Performing Arts across the river, the Pérez Art Museum and Frost Science Museum on the bay at Museum Park, and the Brickell City Centre mixed-use complex with its luxury retail floor, restaurant cluster and indoor-outdoor "climate ribbon" connecting the streets. The neighbourhood is one of the few places in the southern United States where a resident can credibly live a genuinely walking-and-transit-based urban life — to the supermarket, the gym, dinner, the doctor, the cocktail bar, the office — without using a car for days at a stretch, an unusual proposition outside the North-East corridor. The protected Brickell Key island, reachable across a single bridge from the financial district, holds three of the highest-grade residential towers in the district plus the Mandarin Oriental Hotel, a small park and a continuous bayfront walking loop — a residential cluster with the urban density of Manhattan and the climate of the Caribbean.

The international buyer mix in Brickell is heavily Latin American — Brazilian, Argentine, Colombian, Venezuelan, Mexican and Peruvian buyers anchoring the district since the 1980s, joined since 2020 by a substantial wave of North-East US and California buyers relocating for the state tax advantage, and increasingly by European buyers drawn to the year-round climate and the financial-district professional infrastructure. The architectural inheritance is contemporary — most of the prime towers were built between the 2000s and 2020s — and the area is the natural choice for owners who prefer the high-floor city view over the strand-front beach apartment. The walking and cycling infrastructure inside the district is unusually developed for an American city: the Underline linear park is being progressively delivered along the Metrorail viaduct south to Coconut Grove, the Brickell Bay Drive promenade runs along the protected bayfront, and the Metromover automated people-mover loops the district free of charge. The drive from Brickell to Miami International Airport (MIA) is 15–20 minutes; to Miami Beach 15 minutes across the MacArthur Causeway; to Coral Gables 15 minutes south on US-1; to the Design District 15 minutes north. Climate-wise the area runs 20–25°C (high 60s°F to high 70s°F) in winter and 30–33°C (high 80s°F to low 90s°F) in high summer, with the bayfront microclimate giving the protective easterly trade winds direct access to the high-floor apartments. Best for: finance and tech professionals, Latin-American family-office buyers, North-East US relocators and design-led couples who want a cosmopolitan high-rise scene, contemporary architecture with bay or city views, and the year-round urban density of the most internationally connected business district in the southern United States.

Miami Beach

Miami Beach, the seven-mile barrier island running parallel to the mainland and connected to it by four causeways, is the city's iconic Atlantic-strand address and the area whose architecture has the deepest national pedigree. The island divides cleanly into three sub-zones, each with its own character. South Beach, from South Pointe up to roughly 23rd Street, holds the largest concentration of Art Deco architecture in the world — over 800 protected buildings in the National-Register-listed Miami Beach Architectural District, most of them low-rise apartment buildings, hotels and pastel-painted villas from the 1920s through 1940s — and remains the night-time and design centre of the island. Mid-Beach, from 23rd Street up to 63rd, is the residential heart, with the high-rise condominiums of Collins Avenue facing the Atlantic and the Sunset Harbour and Bay Road waterfront blocks facing Biscayne Bay; the Faena District around 33rd Street has emerged as the contemporary art-and-hotel anchor of the middle island. North Beach, from 63rd Street up to the Surfside line, retains the MiMo (Miami Modern) architectural vernacular of the 1950s–1960s — terrazzo lobbies, jalousie windows, butterfly roofs — and is the quietest and most residential of the three.

Miami Beach's cultural anchors are deep. The New World Symphony in Frank Gehry's building on Lincoln Road runs the most active classical-music education programme in the southern United States; The Bass contemporary-art museum sits in a 1930s Art Deco building near the Convention Center; the Holocaust Memorial Miami Beach by Kenneth Treister stands on Meridian Avenue; the Wolfsonian-FIU museum holds a major collection of late-nineteenth and early-twentieth century design and decorative art. The island's culinary calendar — anchored by the long-established Joe's Stone Crab at South Pointe, the contemporary Miami fine-dining wave at Stubborn Seed, Stiltsville Fish Bar, and the Faena District's Los Fuegos by Francis Mallmann — gives the island a genuinely year-round restaurant scene rather than the high-season-only rhythm of some beach destinations. Lincoln Road, a pedestrian commercial street that has run as Miami Beach's main daytime promenade for ninety years, gives the island a rare strolling-shopping-cafe scale; the long boardwalk and beach walk from South Pointe to North Beach offers four miles of continuous protected pedestrian Atlantic frontage.

The international buyer mix on Miami Beach is the most diverse of any single American second-home address — South American (Brazilian and Argentine in particular, drawn since the 1980s), European (a deep French, Italian and German presence dating to the post-war era, with a more recent British and Scandinavian wave), North-Eastern US (New York, Boston and Philadelphia snowbirds, the original 1950s buyer cohort) and increasingly Russian-speaking and Israeli communities. The architectural restoration of the Art Deco District through the 1980s and 1990s — driven by the Miami Design Preservation League and the city's strict historic-preservation review — has kept the pre-war stock genuinely intact, which means a Miami Beach apartment a block from Ocean Drive sits in a streetscape that has been protected for forty years and is materially scarcer today than at any prior point. The drive from Miami Beach to MIA is 20–30 minutes across the Julia Tuttle or MacArthur Causeway; to Brickell 15 minutes; to Bal Harbour 10 minutes; to the Design District 20 minutes. Climate runs 20–24°C (high 60s°F to mid-70s°F) in winter to 30–32°C (high 80s°F) in high summer, with the Atlantic strand running consistently 1–2°C cooler than the mainland city through the high-summer months thanks to the prevailing easterly sea breeze. Best for: Atlantic-strand traditionalists, design-led couples drawn to the Art Deco heritage, North-Eastern US snowbirds, European buyers wanting the iconic Miami address, and multi-generational families who value the beach-and-boardwalk rhythm of an island address ten minutes from the urban core.

Coral Gables

Coral Gables, the south-western residential city embedded inside greater Miami, is the old-money architectural heart of the region — built between 1922 and the late 1920s as one of the most thoroughgoing pre-war planned communities in the United States, with a comprehensive Mediterranean-Revival architectural code still enforced today by the city's Board of Architects. The City of Coral Gables covers about 13 square miles south-west of central Miami, with tree-lined avenues laid out by George Merrick around four signature anchors: the Granada and Alhambra arterial entrances, the historic Venetian Pool carved from a 1920s coral quarry, the Biltmore Hotel with its 1926 tower modelled on the Giralda of Seville, and the long ceremonial axis of Coral Way running east-west through the historic core. The architectural code prescribes tile roofs, stucco facades, arched openings, courtyards and the consistent palette of Mediterranean-Revival materials — which has given the city the most consistent pre-war architectural character in southern Florida.

The Gables cultural calendar runs deeper than its leafy-suburban scale suggests. The Coral Gables Art Cinema is one of the most active arthouse film venues in the southern United States; the Books & Books independent bookshop on Aragon Avenue has anchored a literary calendar that includes the annual Miami Book Fair warm-up events and a year-round author programme; the Lowe Art Museum at the University of Miami holds the largest fine-art collection in southern Florida. The Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden on Old Cutler Road just south of the Gables is the most significant tropical botanical collection in the United States. The Miracle Mile commercial strip and the Giralda Plaza pedestrian zone give the city a walkable restaurant-and-retail scale immediately adjacent to the residential streets. The Biltmore Hotel with its 1926 tower, championship golf course and Italian-style swimming pool anchors the city's hospitality calendar.

The prime residential sub-zones inside Coral Gables divide between the historic core (the streets immediately around the Biltmore, Venetian Pool and the University of Miami, where the original 1920s villas are concentrated), Cocoplum and Gables Estates (the gated waterfront communities on the bay south-east of the core, with private docks on the deep-water canals), Riviera and Granada (the long residential streets running south from the historic core toward Cocoplum) and the Crafts Section (the smaller-lot pre-war residential blocks north of the Biltmore, more domestic in scale). The drive from Coral Gables to MIA is 15 minutes; to Brickell 15 minutes north on US-1; to Coconut Grove 10 minutes; to Miami Beach 30 minutes across the city. Climate runs 19–24°C (high 60s°F to mid-70s°F) in winter to 30–32°C (high 80s°F) in high summer; the deep tree canopy of the historic core — old-growth banyans, royal palms, live oaks — gives the city's interior streets a noticeably cooler microclimate than the more exposed downtown blocks. Best for: classical-architecture enthusiasts, design-led families and empty-nesters who value pre-war Mediterranean-Revival pedigree above the urban high-rise or beach-strand experience, who want the leafy suburban scale immediately south-west of the urban core, and who will use the property in genuinely year-round mode across an extended winter and shoulder season.

Coconut Grove

Coconut Grove, the bayfront village immediately south of Brickell, is the oldest continuously inhabited neighbourhood in greater Miami — older than the city itself, having been settled in the 1870s by Bahamian and New England pioneers around the protected anchorage of Biscayne Bay. The village has retained a bohemian-luxury character rare in the southern United States: deep tree canopies, the historic Peacock Park running down to the bay, the long stone wall and gardens of Vizcaya Museum and Gardens just to the north, the marinas of Dinner Key and Coconut Grove anchoring one of the most active recreational sailing communities on the Atlantic coast. The architecture mixes early-twentieth-century wooden cottages and stone bungalows, mid-century modern bayfront villas, and a recent cluster of contemporary low-rise condominium buildings around the village core — the Park Grove and Grove at Grand Bay developments by international architects have given the Grove a contemporary residential dimension over the past decade without overwhelming the village scale.

The prime sub-zones around Coconut Grove divide between the village core (the streets immediately around CocoWalk and Main Highway, with the most walkable restaurant and retail concentration), the bayfront strip (the streets between South Bayshore Drive and the bay itself, including the highest-priced direct-bayfront villas and the Park Grove towers), the South Grove (the leafy residential area south of the village toward Old Cutler Road, with the largest lots and the deepest tree canopy) and the North Grove (the streets between McFarlane Road and Vizcaya, retaining the bohemian-cottage scale of the original 1880s settlement). The drive from Coconut Grove to MIA is 15–20 minutes; to Brickell 10 minutes north on Bayshore Drive; to Coral Gables 10 minutes west; to Miami Beach 30 minutes via the Rickenbacker Causeway and the MacArthur. Climate runs 19–24°C (high 60s°F to mid-70s°F) in winter to 30–32°C (high 80s°F) in high summer; the bay-front exposure gives the Grove a slightly cooler high-summer microclimate than the inland districts. Best for: sailing families, bohemian-luxury couples drawn to the deep tree canopy and the bayfront village rhythm, multi-generational families who value the walkable village core and the year-round outdoor culture, and design-led owners who treat the Grove's mix of cottage-and-contemporary architecture as the primary destination rather than the urban high-rise or the Atlantic strand.

Edgewater, Design District and Wynwood

The corridor running north of downtown along the bay — Edgewater, the Design District and Wynwood — is the most rapidly transformed part of greater Miami over the past fifteen years, and the area whose property stock has the strongest contemporary-architecture credentials. Edgewater, hugging the bay from the Adrienne Arsht Center for the Performing Arts north to the Julia Tuttle Causeway, has been built out almost entirely in contemporary high-rise residential towers in the past decade — many of them by internationally recognised architects (Ateliers Jean Nouvel, Arquitectonica, Carlos Ott) commissioned to give Miami a contemporary residential dimension that the older Brickell stock did not provide. The Design District, north of Edgewater around 40th Street, has emerged in the past twenty years as the largest concentration of luxury-fashion flagships south of New York — Louis Vuitton, Hermès, Dior, Cartier, Bulgari, Prada and Tom Ford all hold flagships in the district's contemporary mixed-use blocks — pairing with the Institute of Contemporary Art Miami and the de la Cruz Collection as the city's gallery-and-shopping anchor.

Wynwood, immediately west of the Design District around NW 2nd Avenue, is the contemporary-art quarter of greater Miami — the former warehouse and industrial blocks of the 1950s–70s reimagined since the early 2000s as a dense concentration of street-art murals, gallery spaces, microbreweries, design studios and the Goldman family's Wynwood Walls open-air museum. The annual Art Basel Miami Beach in early December — the largest contemporary-art fair in the Americas — pulls international collectors through the Design District, Wynwood and the satellite fairs along the bay, and the surrounding week of openings, parties and events has become one of the city's defining cultural moments. The residential stock in the Edgewater–Design District–Wynwood corridor is predominantly contemporary high-rise condominium, with smaller boutique developments in Wynwood and a handful of restored warehouse-loft conversions. The drive from the corridor to MIA is 15–20 minutes; to Brickell 10–15 minutes south; to Miami Beach 15–20 minutes via the Julia Tuttle Causeway. Climate is comparable to Brickell — 20–25°C (high 60s°F to high 70s°F) in winter to 30–33°C (high 80s°F to low 90s°F) in high summer. Best for: contemporary-art collectors, design-led couples drawn to the new high-rise architectural pedigree, fashion and luxury-retail enthusiasts who value the Design District concentration, and owners who time their stays around the Art Basel December week and the Miami Art Week satellite calendar.

A year in Miami

Spreading 45 days of use across a calendar year is itself a skill — and one of the unsung benefits of Miami specifically is that the city's combination of mild winters, transitional springs, intense summer storms and dry autumn shoulder seasons gives an owner usable weeks across more of the year than almost any other American second-home destination. Below is a walk through the year with the particular weeks owners across the COP Miami portfolio return to most often. The pattern is broadly the same across all eight co-owners of a given property, with the calendar mechanics ensuring every owner gets a fair allocation of peak weeks across a multi-year cycle. Owners who are flexible enough to use shoulder weeks — rather than competing for the prime November-through-April high season — consistently report a higher use-quality from their share than those who insist on peak.

High season (November–April)

The Miami high season is by some measures the longest of any major American second-home destination — six months of reliably mild, dry, sunny weather running from the first cold front of late October through the spring break crowds of early April. November on the coast is already running at 22–27°C (low 70s°F to low 80s°F) in the day, the high-summer rains have finished, and the visitor season opens with the Miami Book Fair International downtown in mid-November and the run-up to Thanksgiving travel. The first real peak of the season is the early-December week of Art Basel Miami Beach — the largest contemporary-art fair in the Americas, occupying the Miami Beach Convention Center for four days and pulling international collectors and galleries through the Design District, Wynwood and the city's satellite fairs and openings for the entire week before. The art-week dinners along Lincoln Road, the Faena District openings, the Pérez Art Museum and Institute of Contemporary Art exhibitions, and the parties at the Edition and Faena hotels turn early December into the most internationally connected fortnight on the Miami calendar.

December and January are the heart of the snowbird season — the long Christmas-and-New-Year window pulling North-Eastern and Mid-Western US families south, the daytime temperatures reliably in the 22–25°C (low to high 70s°F) band, the sea temperatures still above 23°C (73°F) on the strand. The Christmas-week boat parades on Biscayne Bay, the New Year's Eve celebrations across South Beach and downtown, and the long January golf season at Trump Doral and the historic Biltmore course in Coral Gables give the city its high-season urban-resort rhythm. February brings the Miami Open tennis tournament warm-up — the main draw runs in late March at Hard Rock Stadium — the Miami Boat Show in mid-February at the Miami Marine Stadium, and the Coconut Grove Arts Festival on Presidents' Day weekend. March sees the spring-break crowds peaking around the third week, the Miami Open tennis main draw, the Ultra Music Festival downtown at Bayfront Park in the last weekend, and the daytime temperatures climbing toward 27–29°C (low 80s°F). For owners who prefer the dry-air-and-mild-temperatures Miami rather than the high-summer heat, December through March is consistently named the favourite season.

April closes the high season on a slightly quieter note — the spring-break crowds disperse after Easter, daytime temperatures running 26–29°C (high 70s°F to mid-80s°F), sea temperatures past 25°C (77°F) on the strand, and the strand-front restaurant calendars at their last reliably dry-air month before the rain pattern shifts. The April week between spring break and the first summer holiday trickle is one of the secret pleasures of a Miami share — sea warm enough for full swimming, daytime humidity still moderate, the restaurant calendar fully open without the high-season booking pressure.

Transition (April–May)

Late April and May represent Miami's under-used transition window — the daytime temperatures climbing toward 28–31°C (low 80s°F to high 80s°F), sea temperatures past 26°C (79°F), humidity beginning to build but still well short of the high-summer thunderstorm pattern. The high season's restaurant and cultural calendars wind down through May; many of the North-Eastern snowbirds return to summer homes; and Miami enters a genuinely quieter rhythm before the high-summer Latin-American and European visitor wave begins in late June. The city's restaurant scene takes the cue for its annual menu refresh in May, the Miami Spice restaurant promotion runs August through September with the build-up beginning earlier, and the long shoulder weeks of late April through mid-June consistently deliver the best ratio of warm weather, low crowds and full restaurant availability of the entire Miami calendar. For owners with calendar flexibility, this is the most rewarding window of the year — the city is warm, dry, half-empty, and operating at full service.

This is also the window in which the city's sailing and watersports community peaks. The Coconut Grove regatta calendar runs through May, the protected waters of Biscayne Bay sit at their year-round best for sailing, paddleboarding and small-boat use, and the Atlantic strand from Crandon Park down to South Pointe is reliably swimmable from morning through mid-afternoon without the high-summer thunderstorm interruption. May is consistently named the favourite month by Miami owners with active outdoor calendars.

Summer storms (June–September)

The Miami summer pattern is well-defined and worth understanding before allocating weeks. June opens the rainy season — daytime temperatures running 29–32°C (mid-80s°F to high 80s°F), sea temperatures past 28°C (82°F), and the characteristic afternoon thunderstorm pattern beginning. Most summer days in Miami follow a reliable rhythm: clear mornings into early afternoon, cumulus build-up by 2pm, a brief intense thunderstorm between 3pm and 5pm, clearing by 6pm. The city's outdoor calendar adapts: morning beach and bay time, afternoon indoor (the Pérez Art Museum, the Vizcaya gardens, the air-conditioned restaurant scene), evening dining and outdoor walks. The summer pattern is far less disruptive in practice than it sounds — most summer thunderstorms last forty-five minutes and clear to a fresh evening — but it is a real difference from the high-season weather and worth allocating mentally.

July and August are the absolute peak of the rainy season — high temperatures running 31–33°C (high 80s°F to low 90s°F), humidity above 80% by day, daily afternoon thunderstorms, the heat-index regularly above 37°C (100°F). The high-season Northern American visitor base is gone; the city's Latin-American visitor wave (Brazilian, Argentine, Mexican) is at its peak around school holidays from late June through early August; the European August holiday wave is visible from the second week of August onward. Miami in high summer is a different city from Miami in February — a hotter, wetter, more Latin-American-flavoured city — but for owners who prefer the bay-and-pool rhythm to the strand-and-restaurant one, it has its own logic. The protected pool decks of the Brickell towers, the bayfront Coconut Grove villas and the Coral Gables courtyards run at their best in high summer; the air-conditioned cultural calendar (Pérez Art Museum, Frost Science, Faena Forum exhibitions) remains in full operation; and the long late-summer evenings on the strand or the bay are some of the most distinctive of the year.

September brings the climatological peak of Atlantic hurricane season — the historical peak date of 10 September sits at the centre of a five-week window when tropical-storm and hurricane formation is most likely. Most Miami summers see no direct hurricane impact, but September is the month when an owner allocating weeks should expect to monitor the National Hurricane Center forecast and to have a flexible-rebooking contingency in place. The city's hurricane-preparation infrastructure — building codes, storm-shutter requirements for high-rises, the protected bay anchorages — is the most developed in the United States, with the modern Florida Building Code (enacted post-Hurricane Andrew in 1992 and significantly strengthened in 2002) producing residential stock that materially exceeds any other US hurricane-zone construction standard. None of which is a reason to avoid Miami in September, but it is the month when calendar flexibility pays the largest dividend.

Hurricane awareness and autumn (October–November)

For many seasoned Miami-property owners, October and November are the favourite months. October closes the rainy season — daytime temperatures dropping toward 27–29°C (low 80s°F), humidity easing, the daily thunderstorm pattern winding down through the month, sea temperatures still above 27°C (81°F) on the strand. The high-summer visitor base has gone; the high-season Northern American base has not yet arrived; the city's cultural calendar enters its autumn opening (the Miami Symphony, the Florida Grand Opera, the New World Symphony seasons all open in October) and the autumn restaurant scene takes the cooler temperatures off the strand. The Miami Open autumn satellite calendar runs through October, and the long Coconut Grove and Coral Gables outdoor weekend rhythm — farmer's market, sailing regattas, the Vizcaya gardens — picks up to its year-round best.

Hurricane awareness remains a relevant consideration through October — the season runs officially to 30 November and named storms can form into early November in warm-water years — but the climatological probability drops sharply after the third week of October. Owners who track the calendar carefully often time an October stay specifically for the trough between the September peak risk and the first November cold fronts: roughly 15 October to 5 November sits at the year-round sweet spot for warm-water swimming, low humidity, reliably dry conditions, low crowds and full restaurant service. For owners with calendar flexibility, this is the genuinely under-promoted window of the Miami year. The full Atlantic hurricane-season climatology is published by the National Hurricane Center; the city's emergency-preparation guidance is maintained by Miami-Dade Emergency Management and the property's management company will run all routine preparation, evacuation and recovery on the owner's behalf without any owner-side action required.

One pattern worth naming explicitly is the "Miami-as-base" use mode that has emerged in the past decade and that fractional ownership suits particularly well. Florida property tax basics: no state income tax, county property tax, homestead exemption

Florida operates a distinctively favourable property-tax framework for second-home owners relative to almost any other US state, and almost all of the routine compliance is handled through the LLC and its appointed Florida tax adviser rather than by the individual owner. The headline structural advantage is that Florida has no state personal income tax — confirmed in the state constitution since 1924 and reaffirmed at each constitutional convention, with no state estate tax and no state inheritance tax overlay either — making Florida one of the most tax-favourable states in the United States for residents and the only major Sun-Belt state without state income tax under the Florida Department of Revenue framework. The state's revenue is generated through sales tax, property tax and tourism levies rather than income tax, which is why the Northern American snowbird wave south of the past decade has been so structurally consistent.

Miami-Dade property tax is the principal annual cost on the Miami property and is administered by the Miami-Dade Property Appraiser. Property tax in Florida is calculated on the just value (effectively market value, capped under the Save Our Homes assessment limit for homestead-exempt properties) and falls within a typical millage rate of 20–22 mills across the county, depending on the specific municipality (City of Miami, Miami Beach, Coral Gables, Coconut Grove and the unincorporated areas each set their own millage rate within the county framework). Property tax is paid by the LLC from the annual service charge collected from co-owners, so individual owners never deal with the county directly. The Homestead Exemption — which reduces taxable value by up to $50,000 and caps annual increases under the Save Our Homes provision — is available only to Florida primary residences and does not apply to a fractional second home, since the property is not the owners' permanent residence; this is the standard treatment for any Florida second home regardless of ownership structure and is built into the annual carry calculation. The detailed homestead-exemption rules are published by the Florida Department of Revenue.

Beyond the county property tax, the other line items in the Miami carry are hurricane and windstorm insurance (substantially more expensive than general homeowners' insurance in cooler-climate states, and a non-negotiable in the Florida coastal zone), HOA fees in condominium buildings (the dominant cost on Brickell and Miami Beach high-rise stock, with post-Surfside-collapse legislation requiring heavier reserves on older buildings, administered by the Florida Division of Condominiums), tourist development tax on any rental-use nights, and the standard utility-and-management operating budget. All of these are handled at the LLC level by the property's professional management, with the individual owner seeing only the consolidated annual service-charge figure.

Inheritance, Florida probate and the LLC overlay

Directly held Florida real estate is subject to Florida probate jurisdiction on the death of the owner, regardless of where the owner is domiciled — which means a non-US-resident buyer holding a Miami property directly in their personal name would typically face a separate Florida ancillary probate process on death, in addition to the home-jurisdiction succession, with the associated delay, cost and disclosure that implies. The LLC structure handles this materially more cleanly. Because the underlying real estate is held by the LLC rather than by the individual co-owner, the asset that passes on death is the LLC membership interest — which under most bilateral interpretations is treated as movable rather than immovable property, and which therefore passes under the deceased's home-jurisdiction succession law rather than triggering a separate Florida ancillary probate. This is one of the practical structural advantages of the LLC vehicle for international buyers, and one of the reasons it is the preferred holding structure for non-US-resident shared ownership in Florida. As always, the precise treatment is jurisdiction-specific and requires personal tax advice from a qualified adviser in the buyer's home country, and we recommend any international buyer review the specific position with their own counsel before purchase.

Hurricane insurance, building codes and the post-Surfside reserve regime

Two structural features of the Florida coastal property market deserve specific mention because they materially shape the carry on any Miami property and are areas where the LLC and professional management model add the most operational value relative to direct ownership. The first is hurricane and windstorm insurance, which in Florida is split from the standard homeowners' policy and which is materially more expensive than general homeowners' insurance in cooler-climate states. The state-backed insurer of last resort is Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, with the private market regulated by the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation; the legal framework, including the 2022 and 2023 reforms that have stabilised the market, is set by the Florida Legislature. The professional management running each Miami COP property handles the annual insurance renewal, the storm-shutter and impact-glass compliance, and the post-storm claims handling on behalf of the LLC — an operationally meaningful relief from what is otherwise one of the most fiddly aspects of holding a Florida second home directly.

The second is the Florida building code, which has been progressively strengthened since Hurricane Andrew in 1992 and produces residential stock that materially exceeds any other US hurricane-zone construction standard. The Miami-Dade product approval regime — the strictest impact-glass, roofing and structural standard in the United States — gives the city's residential stock built since 2002 a structural resilience that fundamentally changes the hurricane-risk profile compared to older inventory. Combined with the post-2021 SB-4D condominium-safety statute (enacted after the Surfside Champlain Towers South collapse), which mandates milestone structural inspections at 25 or 30 years and reserve-funding requirements for capital works, the Miami high-rise stock today operates under a more rigorous safety-and-funding framework than at any prior point in its history. Still deciding which Miami neighbourhood?

Many readers arrive on this page already half-decided — they want Miami, but not yet which Miami. The choice between Brickell, Miami Beach, Coral Gables, Coconut Grove and the Edgewater–Design District–Wynwood corridor is rarely about budget alone; the major sub-zones sit in similar price bands once you compare like-for-like quality. The decisive question is usage pattern. How will you actually spend your weeks across a calendar year? The honest answer for most buyers is one most have not previously articulated, because the question rarely arises until ownership becomes concrete. Our team has spent years inside the Miami second-home market and can walk you through the regional differences — climate, calendar, owner mix, day-to-day rhythm — before you commit to a zone. Below is the framework we walk through with buyers who reach the same fork, with deliberate over-simplification — most owners actually end up combining elements from more than one — but useful as a starting point.

Choose Brickell if you want a cosmopolitan high-rise scene, contemporary glass-and-steel architecture with bay and city views, the shortest drive in greater Miami from airport to front door, and a year-round restaurant-and-finance-district rhythm that holds up better than the more seasonal sub-zones. Brickell works hardest for owners drawn to the urban high-rise over the strand-front apartment or the residential villa, who value the proximity to the financial district and the protected bayfront walking infrastructure, and who use the property in genuinely year-round mode rather than the winter-only snowbird pattern.

Choose Miami Beach if you want the iconic Atlantic-strand address, the depth of the Art Deco architectural pedigree, the long-established North-Eastern US and European snowbird community, and the seven-mile barrier-island scale that separates the beach experience from the urban core. Miami Beach is the natural choice for design-led couples, multi-generational families who want the strand-and-boardwalk rhythm, and owners drawn to the city's most photographed and most internationally recognisable residential address.

Choose Coral Gables if you want the old-money Mediterranean-Revival architectural pedigree, the deep tree-canopied residential streets, the leafy suburban scale immediately south-west of the urban core, and the pre-war planned-community character that no other Miami sub-zone can match. The Gables works hardest for classical-architecture enthusiasts and design-led families who value architectural pedigree above the high-rise or strand experience, and who use the property in genuinely year-round mode across an extended winter and shoulder season.

Choose Coconut Grove if you want the bohemian-luxury bayfront village, the oldest neighbourhood in greater Miami, the protected sailing waters of Biscayne Bay, and the walkable village core with its mix of cottages, mid-century moderns and contemporary low-rise. The Grove is the natural choice for sailing families, bohemian-luxury couples drawn to the deep tree canopy and the bayfront rhythm, and design-led owners who treat the village's mix of cottage-and-contemporary architecture as the primary destination rather than the urban high-rise or the Atlantic strand.

Choose the Edgewater–Design District–Wynwood corridor if your second-home calendar is built around contemporary art, design and the Art Basel December week, if you value the strongest contemporary-architecture credentials of any greater-Miami sub-zone, and if you want the rapid mid-rise residential transformation of the past decade. The corridor is the right answer for art collectors, fashion-and-design-led owners, and design-led couples who time their stays around the December Art Basel calendar and the satellite cultural week — and, unlike a traditional timeshare which locks you into one week in one property year after year, the easiest Miami address to combine with a second share elsewhere, since the city's year-round usability means it pairs naturally with a seasonal alpine or Mediterranean share.

The decision shortcut: if your dominant use is year-round + finance-district + high-rise, choose Brickell. If it is snowbird + Atlantic strand + Art Deco, choose Miami Beach. If it is pre-war architecture + tree canopy + leafy residential, choose Coral Gables. If it is sailing + bohemian-luxury + bayfront village, choose Coconut Grove. If it is contemporary art + Art Basel + design-led, choose the Edgewater–Design District–Wynwood corridor. If it is two of the above, the multi-share approach is often more economical than scaling up to a single whole property at any one address.

A few cross-cutting themes are worth flagging before the portfolio paragraph. The first is airport proximity: every Miami sub-zone covered above sits within 30 minutes of MIA in good traffic, which is unusual for a major American city and which makes the high-frequency, short-stay use pattern realistic. The second is language infrastructure: every major Miami sub-zone is comfortably bilingual in Spanish and English, and the Latin-American Portuguese, French and Italian communities have full professional infrastructure (legal, medical, financial, school) running in the relevant language, removing the cross-border friction that affects some other US destinations for non-Anglophone buyers. The third is year-round usability: every Miami sub-zone has a usable winter (the structural advantage over any northern American second-home market), a transitional spring, a hot wet summer and a dry autumn — the calendar mix that suits the fractional 6-7 week pattern better than a single-season destination. The fourth is cultural depth: every Miami sub-zone is within a 20-minute drive of the city's full museum, performing-arts, sailing, golf, sports, dining and contemporary-architecture programme — no single sub-zone is culturally isolated, which is unusual for an American second-home market and which gives the fractional 6-7 weeks unusually high optionality.

The portfolio approach is worth at least mentioning. A meaningful proportion of Miami co-ownership owners hold more than one share — either elsewhere in the United States (an Aspen or Vail ski chalet, a 30A beach cottage, a Florida Keys cottage, a Napa or Sonoma wine-country home) or further afield (a French Alps chalet, a Mallorca villa, an Italian Lakes apartment, a Côte d'Azur villa). For owners building a multi-region portfolio with COP, you have one team across every destination — the same advisors, the same calendar mechanics, the same resale process across every property you own. (For a wider orientation across the region's culture, dining and seasonal calendar, the official Greater Miami & Miami Beach tourism site is a useful starting reference.) Two 1/8 shares — a Miami winter-and-shoulder home plus a Colorado or French Alps ski share, say — give an owner roughly 90 days of use across a calendar year, drawn from genuinely different lifestyle modes, at a combined annual carry that is still a fraction of what a single whole property at either address would cost.

Miami vs the other US fractional-ownership destinations is a question we are asked frequently. The honest answer is that Miami pairs naturally with — rather than competes against — most of the other US clusters in the COP portfolio. The Colorado ski destinations (Aspen, Vail, Breckenridge, Park City) are the seasonal opposite of Miami, with December through March as the prime quarter and the wrong half of the year for Miami's high season; the two together cover the calendar. 30A and the Florida Keys are the same-state alternatives — quieter, smaller, more vernacular, with less international infrastructure but more of a beach-town rhythm; an owner choosing between Miami and 30A is essentially choosing between an international urban gateway with a strand and a strand-focused Gulf-coast beach village with less urban depth. California (Lake Tahoe, Napa-Sonoma, Newport Coast, Palm Springs) offers the opposite-coast equivalent of Miami's year-round climate but at materially higher entry prices and at six hours' time-zone difference from Latin America; for buyers whose family or business base is in South America, Miami works better. New York, of course, sits in the other direction entirely — and many Miami owners hold a New York apartment as the cultural counterpart to their Miami strand-or-bayfront home. The fractional model is structurally designed for the multi-region buyer, and Miami is one of the most natural starting points in the portfolio.
