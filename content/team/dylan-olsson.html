A bicultural upbringing

Dylan Olsson grew up between London and Marbella, with family roots that extend across four countries. The bicultural background — Spanish second-home life in the Costa del Sol since childhood, schooled in the UK, with cross-Channel travel as a constant — produced an instinctive familiarity with the international buyer's perspective that defines most of Co-Ownership Property's clientele.

That experience matters operationally because international second-home buying isn't just a transaction — it's a cultural translation. A British buyer's expectations about how a Spanish property purchase should work are usually different from how it actually works. A Spanish notary's view of the same transaction is different again. The buyer who succeeds in cross-border second-home ownership is the one with someone to translate not just the language but the entire operational rhythm — what's expected, what's normal, what's a red flag, what's a routine misunderstanding to navigate around.

The international buyer perspective

The international second-home buyer profile that Dylan focuses on shares some specific characteristics — and understanding them changes which properties and operators end up being the right fit:

  • Multi-jurisdiction logistics. A British buyer purchasing a Spanish co-ownership share is dealing with British work life, Spanish notarial process, possibly a Spanish bank for the IBI tax payments, possibly a Spanish accountant for non-resident tax filings, and the operator's coordination of all of the above. The friction isn't insurmountable but it's real, and operators differ meaningfully in how much friction they absorb on the buyer's behalf.
  • Currency and capital deployment. Most international buyers have base-currency considerations — GBP-USD-EUR mix in particular. Capital deployed in EUR for a Spanish or French property has different opportunity-cost characteristics than capital deployed in USD for a US property, and the choice between European and US operators isn't just about geography; it's about how the buyer's capital is structured.
  • Schengen and residency considerations. The 90-day-per-180-day Schengen rule affects how many days non-EU buyers can realistically use a European property each year. For a 1/8 share entitling roughly 45 days of usage, the Schengen rule is comfortably within the buyer's allocation; for buyers who acquire multiple European shares and want to combine them, the cumulative day count can run up against Schengen limits.
  • Family-stage and usage patterns. A family with school-age children has very different peak-week needs than a retired couple with flexible scheduling. The operators' rotation systems handle peak weeks differently, and the right operator for an Easter-and-August-focused family isn't always the right operator for a shoulder-season buyer.

The COP marketplace voice

Like David, Dylan works across all five COP partner operators rather than representing a single brand. The day-to-day reality of cross-operator sales is that the right answer for a specific buyer often involves comparing several partners against each other — a buyer who walked in thinking they wanted a Pacaso property may end up with a MYNE share because the MYNE inventory in their target Spanish region is significantly deeper, and a buyer who started looking at Vivla may end up with &Hamlet because the &Hamlet property tier matches their preferences better.

This marketplace approach is what makes COP genuinely useful for buyers in a fragmenting category — when there are five major operators with overlapping but non-identical inventories, a buyer working directly with any single operator only sees a fraction of what's actually available. The cross-operator view is what produces better matches.
