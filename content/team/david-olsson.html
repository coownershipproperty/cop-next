The Alpine years

David Olsson spent more than two decades selling premium ski properties across the French Alps before founding Co-Ownership Property in 2022. Over those years he worked with international buyers across more than forty Alpine resorts — Courchevel, Val d'Isère, Méribel, Chamonix, Megève, La Plagne, Tignes, Les Arcs, La Rosière, Saint-Martin-de-Belleville, Les Gets, Morzine, Avoriaz, La Tania, Les Menuires, Val Thorens, Alpe d'Huez, Les Deux Alpes, and the entire 3 Vallées and Paradiski areas. The career started in the late 1990s and early 2000s, when buying a French ski property was genuinely accessible to a wider band of professional buyers — French mortgage rates sat below 2%, terms stretched to 25 years, and Alpine resort prices, while never cheap, still bore a meaningful relationship to Parisian ones.

That world gradually disappeared. Between 2017 and 2022, prices in the most sought-after resorts rose by 30–50%, and in some areas overtook Paris per square metre. The chalet in Méribel, the apartment in Chamonix — these had become cash-buyer territory. Many of the long-standing clients David had worked with for decades — the families who had built relationships with him across multiple properties and multiple seasons — were suddenly priced out of the market they had once been comfortably part of.

The clients I had worked with for years still wanted to buy — they just couldn't afford to anymore. They were simply priced out.

That observation became the founding insight for Co-Ownership Property. The clients hadn't changed; the market had. They could still afford 1/8 of the property they wanted. They just couldn't afford 100%. The solution wasn't to convince them to buy smaller properties or in less desirable resorts — it was to give them genuine ownership of the properties they actually wanted, at the share size that matched their realistic usage.

What 20 years in Alpine real estate teaches you

The long career produced an unusually specific set of expertise — not just about property markets in the abstract, but about the operational details of how international buyers actually transact in the Alps and similar markets:

  • Resort dynamics and which destinations hold value. The patterns aren't subtle once you've watched them across multiple cycles. Resorts with strict planning controls (Courchevel 1850, Val d'Isère's old village) appreciate; resorts that overbuilt or weakened their snow infrastructure (a number of mid-altitude resorts) underperform. Aspect, altitude, ski-domain connectivity, and the strength of the local concierge / property-management ecosystem all matter more than headline marketing.
  • How international buyers actually finance Alpine purchases. French mortgage availability for non-resident buyers shifted significantly post-2008, again post-Brexit, and again as interest rates moved through the 2020s cycle. The realistic financing routes for British, American, and other non-EU buyers are different from what the headline rates suggest.
  • The legal-structuring questions that actually matter. Most buyers focus on whether to buy in personal name vs SCI. The structural choices that genuinely affect outcomes — inheritance planning for international families, capital-gains optimisation on resale, IFI wealth-tax exposure for higher-net-worth buyers — are usually under-thought by buyers and over-simplified by agents who haven't done many transactions.
  • The operational realities of owning a property from afar. The cumulative time and money cost of managing an Alpine property at distance — repair contractors who speak French, snow loading on roofs, the gardener who used to be reliable but isn't anymore, the local management company that's now under-staffed — is the part of ownership that buyers consistently under-budget for. It's also the part where the professional-management model of co-ownership genuinely outperforms the self-arranged model of whole ownership.

The pattern that became clear over twenty years: a meaningful fraction of buyers wanted a luxury Alpine property but had no realistic appetite for the operational complexity of whole ownership. They wanted to ski for six weeks a year, not to manage a property the other forty-six weeks. The fractional model — once structured properly with deeded LLC ownership, professional management, and a real resale market — was what those buyers needed.

Founding Co-Ownership Property

Co-Ownership Property launched in 2022 as a marketplace rather than a single-operator brand. The decision was deliberate: the fractional category had several emerging operators (Pacaso in the US, MYNE Homes in Europe, Vivla in Spain, &Hamlet in select markets, Abitaro newer in the US) and the buyer's actual interest wasn't in choosing between operators — it was in finding the right property in the right destination at the right price.

The marketplace positioning was also informed by the way the Alpine real-estate market had always worked. Buyers don't choose a specific developer first and then a property — they choose a resort, then a property within the resort, then sort out the structural details. The same logic applies in fractional: buyers choose a destination (Mallorca, French Alps, Lake Como, Aspen), then a property within the destination, and the operator's identity is secondary. Co-Ownership Property is structured to support that natural buyer journey by aggregating inventory across operators into a single search experience.

Since 2022, the COP portfolio has grown to more than 330 listed properties across the five major partners (Pacaso, MYNE, Vivla, &Hamlet, Abitaro) spanning 11 countries. The editorial layer — destination buyer's guides, operational comparison pages, country-tax explainers, partner-by-partner profiles — exists because the actual buyer questions aren't just about what's available; they're about how each option works, what each costs, and how each suits different buyer profiles.

The COP marketplace voice

Because Co-Ownership Property sells inventory from multiple partner operators, the editorial voice deliberately stays neutral across partners. The same buyer might be best served by a Pacaso property in Lake Tahoe, a MYNE property in Italy, a Vivla property in Mallorca, and a &Hamlet property in the French Alps — and the COP content layer is structured to help buyers compare across operators rather than push one in particular.

This neutrality is what differentiates COP from operator-owned content (which inevitably positions the operator favourably) and from generic listicle comparison sites (which typically don't have direct sales experience with any of the operators they cover). The combination of marketplace breadth + actual sales experience across operators is the editorial position David built COP around.
