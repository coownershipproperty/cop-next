The short answer: A property-specific LLC is a limited-liability company set up for the sole purpose of holding one specific home. The LLC's only meaningful asset is the property. Fractional buyers hold deeded membership interests in this LLC, which gives them a documented claim on the underlying real estate while keeping their ownership cleanly transferable and isolated from any other LLC's liabilities. The "one LLC per home" design is non-negotiable for serious operators — shared corporate vehicles expose each owner to other properties' debts and complications.

What "property-specific" actually means

A property-specific LLC is incorporated for a single purpose: to own one home. Its corporate filings name the property as its primary asset. Its bank account holds funds related only to that property. Its operating agreement governs only the affairs of that property and its co-owners. No other property, no other ownership group, no shared liabilities sit inside the same vehicle.

Contrast this with a shared corporate vehicle — an operator's holding company that owns multiple homes. In that model, the operator's portfolio sits inside one LLC and buyers hold interests that touch multiple properties indirectly. Shared vehicles are simpler for the operator to administer but expose each owner to risk from every other property in the vehicle.

Why one-LLC-per-home is the structural standard

Four reasons that make property-specific LLCs the only acceptable structure for credible single-property fractional ownership.

1. Liability isolation. If one home has a guest accident, a contractor dispute, an environmental claim — anything that produces legal exposure — the liability is ring-fenced inside that home's LLC. It cannot reach owners of other homes in the operator's portfolio. With a shared vehicle, a claim against one property can theoretically reach assets across the whole vehicle.

2. Clear ownership ledger. The LLC's membership register lists the eight (or however many) owners of that specific home and their proportional interests. No ambiguity, no cross-property complication, no operator discretion over what each member "really" owns.

3. Clean exit if needed. If the owners collectively decide to sell the home, the LLC sells the home and distributes proceeds pro-rata. Simple. With a shared vehicle, exiting one property's worth of equity requires either selling membership interests (illiquid) or unwinding shared structures (complex).

4. Operator-failure protection. If the operator goes out of business, a property-specific LLC continues to own the home — owners just need to appoint a replacement manager. With a shared vehicle, the operator's insolvency directly affects the vehicle's stability and the owners' interests.

How to verify a structure is property-specific

Three questions buyers should ask before purchasing.

What is the name of the LLC that owns this specific property, and where is it registered? A property-specific LLC has a unique name and registration number that the operator can share. The buyer can verify it on the relevant corporate registry (Companies House for UK, Delaware/Nevada Secretary of State for US, etc.).

Can I see the LLC's operating agreement? A credible operator shares the operating agreement with prospective buyers as standard. The agreement should govern only this one property.

Where does this LLC's bank account sit, and who has signing authority? The LLC should have its own bank account, holding funds related only to this property. Co-mingling with operator-wide funds is a warning sign.

Why some operators don't use property-specific LLCs

The honest answer: administrative cost. A property-specific LLC requires its own filings, its own bank account, its own accountant — replicated across every property in the operator's portfolio. Shared vehicles are cheaper to run. For buyers, the cost saving is illusory — the structural risk is far more meaningful than the per-property admin saving.
