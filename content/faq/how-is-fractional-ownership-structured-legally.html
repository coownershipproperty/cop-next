The structure in plain terms

Each home is owned by a small group of co-owners — typically eight, sometimes four or twelve. Each co-owner owns a real share of that one specific home. If you buy a 1/8 share, you own an eighth of the home; buy two, and you own a quarter.

What you own is an asset, not a right to use the home. You can sell your share without anyone having to sell the whole house, you can leave it to your heirs, and its value follows the value of the property.

The legal set-up varies by country and by home

Property law differs from one country to the next, so the way co-ownership is put in place legally is not the same everywhere. Our partner arranges the legal set-up for each home and walks you through it, together with the purchase documents, before you buy. That is the moment to ask any question about how your share is held and protected.

We always recommend that buyers take independent legal advice from a lawyer familiar with property in the home's country. How tax works also depends on the country and the home's legal set-up, which our partner explains before you buy; we recommend independent tax advice too.

Why a formal set-up matters

Three practical benefits of a properly documented co-ownership.

Clear ownership. Each co-owner's share is documented, so everyone knows exactly what they own.

Easier resale. Resale is handled through our partner's resale process, without you having to sell the whole house.

Decision-making framework. The co-ownership agreement defines how decisions get made between co-owners — ordinary matters, special assessments, replacement of management, eventual sale of the home. Big decisions, such as selling the home or major works, are decided together by the co-owners. This formalises what would otherwise be a friction-heavy multi-party negotiation.

What the co-ownership agreement covers

Every credible co-ownership includes a comprehensive agreement between the co-owners. Buyers should read it before purchasing. Key clauses to look for:

  • How usage time is allocated (rotation rules, peak-week priority)
  • How shares can be transferred (operator-controlled vs open-market)
  • Voting thresholds for ordinary decisions vs major decisions
  • How replacement of management is triggered
  • How special assessments are levied and capped
  • What happens if an owner fails to pay their share of fees
  • How and when the home itself can be sold
  • Dispute-resolution procedures between co-owners

One home, one group of owners

Each home is set up on its own, with its own group of co-owners, rather than pooled with other properties. That keeps your share tied to the one home you chose.
