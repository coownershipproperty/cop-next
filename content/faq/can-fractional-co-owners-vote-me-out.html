The short answer: No. Your fractional share is a deeded LLC membership interest — it's property you own. The other co-owners cannot vote to remove you from ownership, no matter how much they'd like to. The only forced-exit scenarios are operating-agreement-defined: extended non-payment of annual fees beyond the default process (typically 6+ months), or wilful breach of the operating agreement of a severity warranting expulsion (extremely rare). In both cases, the process is formal, gives the owner opportunity to cure, and results in resale (not confiscation) of the share with proceeds returned to the owner net of arrears.

Why your ownership can't be voted away

A fractional share is property. Standard corporate law in every developed jurisdiction protects shareholders/members from being stripped of their ownership by other shareholders' votes. The LLC operating agreement reinforces this — it defines what owners can vote on (management, special assessments, sale of the underlying property) but specifically excludes voting another owner out of the LLC.

This is the same protection that applies to any private-company shareholding. If you own 12.5% of a private company, the other 87.5% can't vote to remove your shareholding. They can vote to do things to the company that you might not like, but they can't take your shares away.

The only forced-exit scenarios

Two scenarios in which an owner can be forced out of the LLC, both governed by the operating agreement:

1. Extended non-payment of fees. The default-handling process (see what if a co-owner stops paying fees?) escalates from late fees → suspended booking rights → formal default → forced resale. Forced resale typically only triggers after 6+ months of unpaid fees. The owner gets multiple opportunities to cure throughout. If resale ultimately happens, the proceeds go to the owner net of arrears and resale costs — confiscation isn't the structure.

2. Wilful breach of the operating agreement. Extremely rare. Would require something like: an owner repeatedly violating the booking rules and refusing to comply after warnings; an owner using the property for illegal purposes; an owner taking actions that materially damage the LLC's interests. Even then, the process requires formal notice, opportunity to cure, and a supermajority owner vote — and ultimately results in forced resale, not loss of value.

What other co-owners CAN do

The operating agreement gives co-owners voting rights over specific LLC matters. With sufficient votes (majority or supermajority depending on the matter), co-owners can:

  • Approve special assessments for major repairs
  • Replace the operator/management company
  • Approve sale of the underlying property (typically requiring supermajority, often 75%+)
  • Amend the operating agreement (typically requiring supermajority or unanimous)
  • Approve material changes to the property

These are decisions that affect the property and the LLC collectively — they don't affect individual owners' ownership of their shares.

What protects you from majority pressure

Three structural protections. First, voting thresholds — major decisions require supermajority (often 75% or higher), so even a majority faction can't unilaterally force material changes. Second, the operating agreement is amendable only under defined rules — typically requiring unanimous or near-unanimous consent for material changes. Three, owners have legal recourse to challenge improper LLC decisions through standard corporate dispute processes.

The eventual sale scenario

One scenario worth covering directly. If a supermajority of co-owners vote to sell the underlying property (e.g. 6 of 8 owners), the LLC sells the home and distributes proceeds pro-rata to all owners — including the minority who voted against. This isn't "voting you out" — it's the LLC's defined process for eventual sale. The dissenting minority receives full fair-market value of their share via the property sale proceeds. They don't get to keep the property against the majority's wishes, but they don't lose value.

This is the same way condominium and shared-ownership structures handle eventual sale across real-estate law generally.

What you should ask before purchase

Three questions. What is the LLC's operating-agreement voting threshold for sale of the underlying property? What are the dispute-resolution mechanisms for owner-owner disagreements? What are the documented forced-exit scenarios and their thresholds?

The honest summary

Fractional ownership is structurally safer than informal joint ownership in this respect: your share is deeded property protected by corporate law. The other co-owners cannot strip you of it. They can collectively make decisions about the property and the LLC under defined rules, but those decisions can't reach inside your individual ownership stake.
